# Why You Shouldn't Put Your Pawns on Your Bishop's Colour

There are two reasons. **The second one is the one that actually loses games.**

I measured it: same material both times, the only difference is which colour squares
the pawns sit on.

| | Own pawns on the bishop's colour | Squares the bishop can move to | Squares controlled by pawns + bishop together |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Pawns on the bishop's colour** | 3 | **3** | 9 (dark 0 · **light 9**) |
| **Pawns on the other colour** | 0 | **9** | 14 (dark 5 · light 9) |

---

## Reason 1: your own pawns are standing in the bishop's way

**A bishop only ever touches one colour of square. Forever.** A light-squared bishop
can reach 32 squares and can never, in its entire life, reach the other 32.

So every pawn you put on a light square crosses one square off the only list your
light-squared bishop has. In the test above: **three pawns on the bishop's colour cut
its available squares from 9 down to 3.**

And **pawns only move forward — they can never retreat.** A piece in the way can step
aside later. A pawn in the way is a permanent wall.

---

## Reason 2: the other colour becomes a set of holes — this is the bigger problem

Look at the last column.

- Pawns on the **opposite** colour: the pawns cover 9 light squares, the bishop covers
  5 dark ones. **Both colours are guarded.**
- Pawns on the **same** colour: the pawns cover 9 light squares and the bishop also
  only covers light squares. **Dark squares covered: zero.**

Pawns and bishops are **complementary**. Pawns capture diagonally, so pawns standing
on light squares guard dark squares. A light-squared bishop guards light squares. Put
them on opposite colours and the whole board is covered.

**Put them on the same colour and the other half of the board becomes a permanent
hole.** Enemy knights, bishops and queens can walk in and sit there, and you cannot
chase them away — your pawns can't reach those squares and neither can your bishop.

### This is exactly what happened in both of your games

Black's pawns were all on light squares, and the only bishop Black had left was the
light-squared one. **So every dark square was free real estate.**

- In the first game White's knights parked on e5 and c6.
- In the second, White's dark-squared bishop travelled f4 → h2 → d4 and delivered the
  final blow from d4.

**Black had no piece that could touch any of those squares, for the entire game.**

---

## How to remember it

> **Pawns guard one colour, the bishop guards the other. That way nothing is left open.**
> Pile them both onto the same colour and you get a bishop with nowhere to go *plus*
> half the board handed to your opponent.

**The practical check:** before you push a pawn, count how many of your pawns are
already on your bishop's colour. **Four is the warning line.**

---

## But this is a guideline, not a law

Two honest exceptions, worth knowing so you don't over-apply it:

1. **When the pawns are doing defensive work.** If your opponent has a light-squared
   bishop, putting your pawns on light squares blocks it. A "bad" bishop is a fair
   price for that — there's a saying that bad bishops protect good pawns.
2. **In closed positions.** If the position is locked and there are no open lines, a
   bishop wasn't going to do much anyway, so the cost is small.

The genuinely fatal combination is the one in these two games: **an open position, a
bishop walled in by its own pawns, and holes all over the other colour.**
